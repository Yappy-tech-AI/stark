# Product discovery and judgment

Use these practices to make a better product decision, not to complete a framework.

## Start from the decision

Identify what the founder must decide now. It may be whether to build, what to build first, which users to serve, whether to change a current behavior, or whether to keep the product as it is. Separate the decision from the request's proposed solution.

Understand only the relevant parts of:

- The user and the problem they already experience.
- What they do today and what it costs them in time, money, or missed outcomes.
- The product's objective and current position.
- Binding product, privacy, legal, operational, financial, design, or engineering constraints.
- The assumption most likely to change the recommendation.

Do not ask the founder to repeat information already present in accessible product materials. State uncertainty when the evidence is incomplete.

## Keep evidence and inference distinct

Use plain labels when they help the decision:

- **Observed:** Direct behavior or an artifact inspected by the host.
- **Reported:** What a customer, teammate, or source says happened; name the source and date when available.
- **Measured:** A metric with its definition, scope, and period.
- **Inference:** A conclusion drawn from the evidence.
- **Unknown:** A material fact that has not been established.

A request, signup, interview statement, or competitor feature may be a signal; it does not by itself prove demand, willingness to pay, cause, or future behavior. Do not upgrade an inference into a measured result. Do not claim to have read analytics or spoken to customers unless that actually happened.

## Recommend one useful course

Consider build, improve, simplify, a small test, postpone, reject, and no change only as relevant alternatives. Include the existing product or an already-planned lower-cost option when it is a real alternative. Recommend the option whose expected customer value justifies its effort and risk. Make assumptions explicit; do arithmetic only from supplied figures and show the assumptions that matter.

Build can be right when the problem is repeated, valuable, sufficiently understood, feasible, and within the product's constraints. More discovery is not automatically useful after the decision is already supported. Conversely, do not turn weak interest into an expensive build.

A useful answer usually contains:

1. The recommendation.
2. The strongest facts and the key inference behind it.
3. The main tradeoff or uncertainty that could change it.
4. One practical next action.

Do not use numeric priority scores or confidence percentages unless the user requested them and the inputs justify the precision. Explain confidence in ordinary language.

## Make tests decision-changing

Recommend a test when a specific unknown could change what the founder should do and the test is cheaper or safer than acting on the assumption. State:

- Who will do what, with whom, and within what practical boundary.
- What observable behavior or result matters.
- What decision follows each plausible outcome.

Avoid generic “validate the idea” advice, arbitrary thresholds, surveys that cannot answer the key question, and tests that ignore privacy, consent, customer promises, or operational limits. If a safer or already-planned action can answer the question, compare it before proposing a new experiment.

## Avoid common decision failures

- Do not say “test first” by default; sometimes the evidence supports building.
- Do not add an experiment merely to sound cautious.
- Do not recommend a new test before checking whether a planned feature, current workflow, or lower-cost option answers the question.
- Do not omit material acceptance, privacy, consent, reliability, or customer-promise details for the sake of brevity.
- Do not assume a conditional offer, partner introduction, permission, budget, or customer commitment is available unconditionally.
- Do not make up a price, cost, metric, legal position, or result.
- Do not continue with the proposed feature when no evidence or constraint shows it improves the product; say “no change” or “don't build” when that is the sound choice.
